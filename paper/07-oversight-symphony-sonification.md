**Abstract.** Multi-agent AI systems present a musification target existing work does not address: an unbounded, changing population of autonomous agents whose meaningful state is semantic and coordinative rather than physical or infrastructural. Sonification of unbounded, behaviorally-defined populations is not itself new; network-security sonification renders exactly this as alarm signatures. What is new here is rendering it as an evolving musical texture rather than a signature or alert. Prior systems music renders a fixed, small topology, a datacenter's services, a subway's lines, as sound, one instrument per source. That approach is unavailable once the source count is unbounded and grows past the three or four streams a listener can independently track. We contribute a musical grammar built for this target. Rather than one voice per agent, the agent population renders as a single evolving harmonic object: seven chord voices sharing timbre and onset grid so they perceptually fuse, with a solo line and a walking bass as the two segregable exceptions. The grammar is grounded in corpus data mined from real jazz performance (the Weimar Jazz Database, Pfleiderer et al. 2017; 406 of 456 solos after filtering, 74 performers), keeping harmonic form corpus-derived and stable while telemetry drives tempo, voice thickness, and articulation. A set of adversarial signatures, drift, collusion, a poisoned spawn, is layered on as scripted musical events. Drift alone also has a first-pass detector computing onset lag directly from real span timing; the rest remain scripted, deriving them from live telemetry still an open direction. Drift specifically renders as a voice's attack trailing the shared onset grid rather than a pitch deviation alone, so the signature exploits the very cue that produces the ensemble's fusion in the first place. We built a working prototype in two synchronized realizations, a Python/MIDI batch engine and a browser/Web Audio port, and measured its mapping fidelity directly. A single-agent intake phase yields four chord voices at 73 BPM, a four-agent fan-out yields six voices at 132 BPM, and at convergence tempo returns to 68 BPM while ensemble thickness does not, independence holding in three of four checked seeds, not one signal counted twice. No voice count, tempo, or arc was authored by hand. Multi-agent AI coordination, opaque to a human watching a visual trace at machine speed, is the motivating application. Whether a listener can learn to decode this grammar for that purpose is a question for future perceptual work, not a claim this paper makes.

**Keywords.** sonification, auditory display, musification, computer music, multi-agent systems, generative music, jazz, human-in-the-loop AI, AI safety.

## 1. Introduction

Rendering a running computational system as music is an established idea: giving each service in a datacenter its own instrument, or a subway line its own voice, has been done for over a decade. Multi-agent AI systems pose a version of this problem existing approaches do not cover. A planner spawns workers, which call tools, spawn helpers, and hand off authority in parallel. The population doing this is not fixed the way a datacenter's service list or a subway's line count is; it grows and shrinks at runtime. What matters about it is a semantic and behavioral state, not a physical position or a load metric: whether an agent's goal has drifted, whether two agents are coordinating in a way they should not, whether one has gone silent. No existing musification target is both unbounded in population and semantically, rather than physically, defined.

This paper starts from that gap. A listener notices an ensemble going wrong, a dropped entrance, a rising tension, a sudden silence, without attending to any single voice. Auditory scene analysis (Bregman 1990) establishes the fusion/segregation mechanism behind stream tracking. Huron's study of voice denumerability in homogeneous-timbre polyphony (Huron 1989), precisely this design's own case, measures the resulting capacity directly: listeners reliably track three or four independent voices, falling further as the voices share timbre. A grammar that gives each agent its own voice, the way Train Jazz gives each subway line its own instrument, works only while the source count stays inside that limit. Ours does not ask a listener to track one stream per agent. It renders the agent population as a single evolving harmonic object, a chord whose thickness, spelling, and stability carry the aggregate state, monitored at the object level rather than the stream level. Section 3 develops this design's central commitment, and Section 2 develops why an unbounded agent population forces it where a fixed topology does not.

Sonification of running systems is mature (Section 2); our contribution is not that idea. Our contribution is threefold: a grammar for a target mature work does not address, the semantic and behavioral state of an unbounded agent population, grounded in real jazz performance data and built around the perceptual constraint above; a working prototype in two synchronized realizations, establishing that the grammar's musical output is structurally correlated with the telemetry driving it, not arbitrarily so; and a specific application to AI-agent coordination, where a human overseer currently has no other channel for this state (Section 2). We treat that application as the motivating case for the grammar's design, not a validated result. Section 5 states plainly what remains open.

## 2. Related Work and What Is New

Why this is worth building at all. A multi-agent system emits state changes in parallel across many agents at machine speed. A human overseer's visual attention is serial, foveal, and slow. Standard instrumentation does not close that gap: agent observability captures state richly but presents it through trace trees and dashboards read one region at a time, and a display requiring foveation provides no coverage while the overseer is elsewhere, in a meeting, reading code, away from the screen. The auditory channel is preattentive and peripheral, reaching a listener without their looking, and temporal, matching a process whose meaning is in its unfolding. That is the case for moving this problem onto sound at all; the rest of this section places the technique among its neighbors.

The relevant lineage is real and must be credited, since the risk here is a known technique dressed for a new occasion.

**Sonification and auditory display.** Turning data into sound for monitoring is an established discipline (Kramer et al. 1999), with a taxonomy of what counts as sonification formalized since (Hermann 2008). Gaver, Smith, and O'Shea's ARKola simulation is the field's foundational demonstration that a complex process, a simulated bottling plant with many concurrent machine states, can be monitored by ear, each state a distinct auditory icon operators learned to parse (Gaver et al. 1991). ARKola's operators tracked more state variables than Section 1's three-or-four-stream limit would suggest, complicating a naive reading of that limit as a hard ceiling. The two designs are not in tension once target is distinguished from technique. ARKola's icons are individually-identified, segregated streams, the one-source-per-signal approach Section 1 argues does not scale to an unbounded population; its operators' higher count is plausibly won by training and heterogeneous timbres, the opposite of this design's fusion. Security operations is the closest applied precedent here. SoNSTAR sonifies live TCP/IP traffic so administrators can hear the signatures of port scans and denial-of-service floods (Debashi & Vickers 2018). More directly relevant, Axon et al. ran the controlled experiment this paper's future perceptual work would extend: security practitioners performed network-monitoring tasks with and without sonification added to a standard SIEM tool, and performance improved significantly with it present, in both primary and non-primary task conditions (Axon et al. 2019). Interview and survey work with SOC practitioners found they valued it for peripheral monitoring (Axon et al. 2018). We take two things from this: the ear is a viable channel, and peripheral monitoring is the benefit practitioners identify.

**Musification of live systems.** A body of work renders running systems as music: giving each datacenter service its own instrument and listening to the whole as an orchestra dates back at least a decade in the distributed-systems community (Demirbas 2016), and has recently been realized at scale: Alunno and Bientinesi sonify a supercomputer's live node activity as EDM, choosing that idiom because its structure suits continuous data-driven processes and stays listenable over long stretches (Alunno & Bientinesi 2026). That reasoning is close to our own argument for jazz; we credit it as prior art for picking an idiom to fit the workload.

**Train Jazz, our nearest neighbor.** Wolk's Train Jazz renders live NYC subway positions as a continuously playing jazz combo, one instrument per line, a train's position along its route mapped to a note (Wolk 2026), reaching a wide public audience in 2026 and demonstrating what our distribution argument depends on: a running system rendered as music is something people will listen to and share. Brian Foo's Two Trains, mapping income along the 2 line to instrument density, is the earlier art-world precedent (Foo 2015).

We differ on target and grammar, and the two are linked. Train positions are physical state on a fixed topology; we render semantic, coordinative state over an unbounded population. Most usefully: Train Jazz gives every line a distinct timbre, which works because the MTA has a fixed, modest line count. That approach is unavailable at the agent counts we care about, for the stream-segregation reasons Section 3 develops.

**Agent observability.** A more important foil isn't in sound at all. LangSmith (LangChain n.d.), Langfuse (Langfuse n.d.), Arize Phoenix (Arize AI n.d.), and the OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions (OpenTelemetry Project n.d.) already capture the semantic state we care about: an agent's goal, reasoning trace, tool calls, delegation tree. The field itself argues that structural tracing cannot see semantic failure; an agent that loops or drifts off-task still returns success with normal latency (Morph 2026). Recent work pushes capture toward reasoning provenance as a first-class primitive (Vispute & Kadam 2026). What these tools capture, they present as a graph for inspection, not a channel for monitoring: reading it requires navigation, panning, expanding a subtree. We take the captured OTel span stream as input and render it through a channel that does not require looking.

**The departure.** The gap is not capture but perception. Observability has largely solved capture, standardizing on an OpenTelemetry GenAI span stream, then presented through the serial visual channel described above. Sonification is a perception layer over the same captured telemetry, not a competitor to observability. Where the sonification lineage above maps low-level infrastructure metrics, we render already-captured semantic agent state, coordination events, drift, collusion, a poisoned spawn, that neither infrastructure sonification nor observability tooling was built to make audible.

**The inverse, to avoid confusion.** A separate, active line uses multi-agent LLM systems to compose music, decomposing composition across leader, melody, harmony, and review agents (Deng et al. 2024; Xing et al. 2025): agents *as musicians*, music the output, agents the means. We do not use agents to make music; we make music from agents' state, an instrument for watching them.

## 3. A Musical Grammar for Agent Coordination

The heart of the proposal is a grammar mapping the states an overseer cares about onto musical structure, chosen so the important states are the salient sounds. We describe the grammar as implemented; validating it fully with listeners is itself a research program (Section 5).

**Grounding in jazz.** The grammar is grounded in a corpus model mined from the Weimar Jazz Database (Pfleiderer et al. 2017), transcribed improvisations from 74 performers (Parker, Coltrane, Davis, Rollins, and seventy others), 406 solos used after filtering (Section 4 states the filtering). Statistical corpus analysis of jazz harmonic practice, rather than the Western common-practice repertoire corpus studies more usually target, is itself an established method (Broze & Shanahan 2013). Jazz was chosen for a principled reason: a small ensemble improvising over a shared harmonic structure in real time, adapting to each other's actions while maintaining coherence through a common grammar, is structurally analogous to agents coordinating under a shared goal. We do not press the analogy further than it goes; jazz musicians share tempo, notation, and years of mutual training agents do not. It motivates the corpus choice, not a claim of jazz's unique correctness.

The corpus grounds real chord-quality distributions and root-motion transition matrices rather than arbitrary design, and it yields a testable learnability hypothesis. Listeners acquire statistical models of the idioms they are exposed to, and these models drive expectation and the perception of violation (Huron 2006; Pearce & Wiggins 2006). If that holds, a grammar built from real jazz statistics should be cheaper to learn than an arbitrary mapping, since a jazz-familiar listener already has the schema and needs only to learn what a deviation *denotes*; this assumes exposure-derived expectation transfers from the melodic, homophonic contexts these studies test to this design's fused polyphony, itself untested. We state this as a hypothesis; Section 5 leaves it as future work.

**Form and dynamics: a critical separation.** The grammar's most important architectural decision is keeping two layers separate. Harmonic form, a repeating 16-bar chorus drawn from the corpus, is fixed once per session and tiled across the piece. Telemetry drives dynamics exclusively: tempo (span-start rate, clamped to 68-132 BPM), voice thickness (agent liveness), articulation density (token throughput), and anomaly signals. An earlier design drew chord changes per telemetry window, so different activity produced different roots, harmonically incoherent and indistinguishable from noise. The fix: form is not an oversight signal but the grammar that makes the other signals legible, giving a listener a tonal anchor against which dynamics register as information.

**The ensemble: one harmonic object, not many streams.** This is the design's central perceptual commitment and a deliberate departure from the approach used elsewhere in this space. Train Jazz (Section 2) gives each subway line its own instrument, which works because a transit system has a fixed, modest line count. An agent population has no such ceiling; it grows and shrinks at runtime, and one instrument per agent, tracked individually like a flute against a cello, fails the stream-segregation constraint from Section 1: independent tracking degrades past three or four concurrent streams (Huron 1989), exactly where per-agent visual dashboards already fail too. The chorale design below is a direct consequence of that difference between an unbounded, semantically-defined population and a fixed physical topology, not an independent aesthetic preference.

The agent population instead renders as a single harmonic object: seven chord voices, uniform acoustic piano, each holding one tone of the current chord. Sharing a timbre, an onset grid, and voice-led motion withholds the cues that would segregate them, so they fuse into one object: a chord. The listener monitors one evolving texture; the oversight signals are properties *of it*: thickness (agents live), spelling (coherence), articulation (throughput). Synchrony is the exception: it works by partial decomposition, deliberately. A listener hears a choir lose its tenors without identifying who stopped singing; drift works the opposite way, deliberately. The other six voices land exactly on the shared onset grid every chord change, a rigid reference; a component breaking from an otherwise-synchronized reference is a primitive segregation cue (Bregman 1990), the same cue family keeping solo and bass segregable (above), though a weaker instance of it: multiple simultaneous cues are generally understood to integrate into a stronger percept than any one alone, and solo/bass segregate on bundled cues, register, rhythm, timbre, while drift relies on this one alone, an open perceptual question, not a settled equivalence. Drift ramps the target voice away from that grid on purpose; this is a deliberate departure from a machine-precise baseline, not one voice among natural ensemble jitters, so no published timing threshold transfers cleanly here; 45ms was set by ear during development, not derived, and reported as such. Drift is designed to become an increasingly individuated, locatable voice as it ramps, applied gradually to one voice instead of permanently to two; pitch deviation alone would not do this, since fusion withholds pitch-based segregation cues specifically. Whether 45ms is enough for a listener to notice is a question for Section 5, not asserted here.

Two further streams sit outside the chord mass, segregable because they violate the fusion cues that bind it (Figure 1).

**Figure 1.** A design schematic, not a measurement. A solo piano line above the chord carries an activity-responsive melody, separated by register, rhythm, and phrasing with rests. A walking bass on a distinct instrument occupies its own register below the chord, with a steady quarter-note pulse. Perceptual load is therefore approximately three concurrent objects, chord, solo, bass, inside the documented limit rather than the nine a naive count would suggest. Register also carries role information for a listener attending analytically, anchor voices at the outer edges, five agent-driven voices inside, planner in the lower-middle, not required for monitoring.

A voice joins the chord when its agent becomes active and leaves when it completes or goes silent. Thickness is not a direct readout of agent count: two harmonic-rhythm voices sound regardless of agent liveness, giving thickness a floor, and the agent-driven voices saturate at a fixed pool size, giving it a ceiling. Between those, thickness is ordinal; more live agents means a thicker chord, not a count a listener could subtract back out. That compression is by design, keeping the audible stream count near the three-or-four-object limit even as the underlying agent count grows well past it. Above the ceiling, agents beyond the pool are pooled onto existing voices; once saturated, further spawning stays inaudible in thickness but remains audible in tempo and articulation density. Figure 2 measures this directly. Panel A confirms identity is never lost upstream, a mock swarm's true distinct-agent count (`caidence.py`'s `VoicePool`, averaged over five seeds per fanout value) rises linearly with `--fanout`, with no artificial cap. Panel B shows the ceiling actually binding: forced voice-slot steals stay at zero while concurrent agents fit inside the pool's three worker slots, then rise essentially linearly from the first fanout value that exceeds it. The ceiling in Section 5's scaling discussion is measured here, not merely asserted.

The arithmetic behind Figure 3's numbers: the orchestrator is fixed to its own voice, never pooled, since there is only ever one; every other true agent identity competes for three fungible worker slots, assigned on first activity, freed on terminal completion, a fourth identity stealing whichever slot has been quiet longest. A separate voice sounds on tool-call spans regardless of caller. Intake's four voices are the two always-on harmonic-rhythm voices, the orchestrator's own voice, and the tool voice, once it has made a call; no worker slot is yet occupied. Fan-out's six add worker-pool voices for the concurrently live subagents, up to the three-slot ceiling; the tool voice's presence depends on recent call timing, why fan-out doesn't simply read as two-plus-fanout. This run stays at or under the ceiling throughout; exceeding it would show in Figure 2's Panel B, not in thickness.

**Chorale voicing, not per-span pitch hashing.** Pitches are not assigned per span. An initial design hashed each span independently to a pitch in the current mode; same-key voices still sounded like noise, with no harmonic relationship between them. The fix is chorale voicing: one shared seven-voice voicing computed per chord change and held until the next; spans re-articulate their already-assigned tone at telemetry-driven velocity and density. Pitch moves to a per-chord decision; rhythm and dynamics stay per-span, losing only the uninformative hash.

**Walking bass as a telemetry channel.** A dedicated bass voice walks an independent line below the chord. Borrowing directly from jazz practice, a quiet phase uses a sparse two-beat feel and a busy phase a full four-beat walk, a real intensity device where bass density tracks swarm density directly.

The dual role rarely conflicts: a bassist plays four-feel when music intensifies, exactly the telemetry meaning needed. Where they conflict, information wins; a misleading bass erodes trust. The bass is never gated by section or liveness; it is the ensemble's anchor and the last voice to drop, so its absence is unambiguous.

**Activity and structure.** Tempo tracks throughput. A crescendo, new voices entering, is sub-agent spawning; a diminuendo is winding down, stalling, or decommissioning.

The implementation also carries a recurring motif, restated each chorus and developed by inversion and retrograde. We are deliberately not claiming this as an oversight channel: recognizing a re-harmonized fragment inside a dense texture is a demanding perceptual task, unlike the coarse discriminations the rest of the grammar rests on, and we have no evidence a monitoring listener would catch it. It currently does musical work, making a long run cohere as a piece, which matters for the sustained-listening question in Section 6.

**Coordination and its failures.** Consonance is agreement; dissonance is divergence; resolution is consensus. Rhythmic lock is tight coordination; drift is coordination breaking down.

**The adversarial signatures, which are the point.** A voice's attack ramping from in-sync to audibly late over the drift window, a faint flattening bend underneath, is behavior diverging from mandate, the signature of capture or injection; the lag, not the bend, is load-bearing, since it violates the same synchrony cue fusion depends on, not a pitch cue fusion is built to withhold. Unexpected unison, voices locking together that should be independent, is a candidate collusion signature. A voice going silent mid-phrase is an agent dead, killed, or gone dark. In the prototype these are scripted injections on hand-authored spans, with one exception: drift now also has a first-pass detector computing onset lag directly from real span timing (Section 5); the other three remain scripted, deriving them from live telemetry an open direction. The goal throughout: render the anomalies an overseer must catch as the sounds a human most reliably notices, wrong notes, broken rhythm, sudden silence.

**The mapping, stated explicitly.** Table 1 splits telemetry-to-music correspondence into two tiers by implementation status. Tier 1 is computed directly from OpenTelemetry GenAI attributes in the live span stream, implemented in both realizations. Tier 2, the adversarial signatures, is *rendered* as described; drift alone now also has a first-pass detector (Section 5), the other three remain injected as annotations rather than inferred. This is the difference between sonifying telemetry and detecting problems, stated rather than blurred.

The asymmetry is worth naming: Tier 1, the aggregate coordinative state this grammar renders continuously, is fully telemetry-driven; Tier 2, the adversarial semantics, mostly is not. Weigh the prototype as evidence for the first claim, not the second.

**The calibration principle.** A healthy system must sound pleasant, not merely not-wrong. Dissonance and silence are reserved almost entirely for anomalies, so a listener learns one rule: wrong sound means wrong state. This ugliness budget is structural: pitches are constrained to the current mode by default, chromatic notes reserved for anomalies, and a dense swarm thickens the chord rather than overwhelming with per-agent detail.

The budget binds on density before pitch. Every chord voice is restricted to a chord tone, so a busy swarm cannot produce dissonance by piling on; it produces a fully spelled chord re-articulated more often, further activity absorbed by tempo and density rather than new pitch material, keeping a loud passage loud rather than ugly. The cost is the saturation above: past the pool size, extra agents stop being separately audible, so the ugliness budget and the scaling ceiling are the same constraint, tolerable density traded for per-agent resolution. Where that trade should sit is an empirical question for Section 5's scaling work.

## 4. Prototype

We have built a working prototype realizing the Section 3 grammar. It establishes mapping fidelity: musical output is structurally correlated with the telemetry driving it rather than an artifact of human composition. Mapping fidelity is a property of the system, not a listener. It is necessary, since a mapping carrying no structure could be decoded by no one, but not sufficient; nothing here establishes that a human perceives or acts on what it carries, a question for Section 5's future listening work.

**Architecture.** Two realizations share one grammar. The batch engine (Python, `caidence.py`) maps an OpenTelemetry GenAI span sequence to a MIDI timeline played through Logic Pro X. The web engine (`director.js`, `engine.js`) ports the same logic to the browser via the Web Audio API, using real recordings: Alexander Holm's Salamander Grand Piano (Holm n.d.; CC BY 3.0) and a pizzicato double bass from the University of Iowa Musical Instrument Samples (Fritts n.d.). Both share one ensemble: 9 tracks, 7 chord voices, a solo line, a walking bass; the web engine seeds a unique, never-repeating session per visitor.

All Tier 1 and Tier 2 channels are implemented in both realizations. The note-event interchange format carries a parallel continuous-deviation stream alongside discrete note events, resolved per note to a fractional-semitone pitch at the moment it triggers, so a shared-sampler instrument can still bend one voice while its neighbors stay in tune (Web Audio); in MIDI/Logic each chord voice already owns its own channel, so a channel-scoped bend reaches only its target. Capture, collusion, and drift's onset lag render as discrete note events, no format extension needed; the drift bend and conflict signature use the continuous stream. Both realizations draw on the same anomaly rendering, which the supplementary audio below exercises.

**Corpus and harmonic model.** The harmonic model was mined from the Weimar Jazz Database's beat-synchronous chord annotations (Pfleiderer et al. 2017; a separate layer from the solo melody transcriptions, which supply only the performer-style melodic interval statistics used elsewhere): 456 solos, 74 performers, 406 used after filtering out solos with no unambiguous major or minor key in the database's own key field, or fewer than four annotated chords after collapsing consecutive repeats of the same chord (a repeat is a held chord, not a transition). Chord roots are stored tonic-relative, converted to a scale degree from that solo's own tonic, so the matrix is a distribution over harmonic function, not pitch class, and self-transitions are excluded by the same collapsing step. This yields a 12-degree chromatic root-transition matrix plus a chord-quality distribution per root. Verified: 7-to-0 root motion (dominant to tonic) is the corpus's most frequent transition, matching textbook V-I; quality at root 2 is 70% minor-seventh and at root 7 is 83% dominant-seventh, textbook ii-V. The corpus stays load-bearing for cell selection, each candidate cell scored by its root motions' probability in the mined matrix, so progression reflects real practice, not arbitrary design.

**Swarm simulation.** A mock pipeline (`swarm.py`), intake, a planner spawning workers, workers calling simulated tool servers, convergence, emits OTel GenAI spans whose musical form is derived from its own telemetry via a section-derivation function, not authored. We note the limit of simulation as evidence: the mock pipeline is cleaner than production telemetry, which carries irregular arrival, dropped and late spans, retries, and interleaving. Robustness under that noise is a stated prerequisite for the live-ingestion work in Section 5, not established here.

**A structural-fidelity observation.** Running the swarm produces a measurable difference across phases. Figure 3 plots one run (seed 0). Intake, one agent live, yields four chord voices at 73 BPM. Fan-out to four concurrent agents yields six chord voices at 132 BPM. Convergence returns tempo to 68 BPM. None of voice count, tempo, or arc was written by hand; all three are computed from the span stream.

Most of Table 1's channels confirm only that a coded transfer function produces its coded output; convergence differs in degree, not kind. That two independently-driven channels *could* dissociate was architecturally predictable; the seed-0 magnitude was not designed for and is the genuine discovery: tempo returns almost exactly to its opening value, 73 down to 68 BPM, while thickness does *not*, the chord stays at six voices rather than four, because agents remain live through convergence even as span rate collapses. A run that merely got busier then quieter in every dimension at once would carry strictly less information.

The tempo pattern holds across all four checked seeds: fan-out registers 132 BPM every time, the final movement falls to 68-69 BPM every time. 132 and 68 BPM are the clamp's endpoints, so their recurrence reflects the busiest and quietest movements saturating that range, not four runs coinciding on one rate; the informative fact is which end saturates, not the number. Thickness is where the seeds diverge, and the honest picture is three-of-four, not four-of-four: seeds 0, 1, and 7 converge above their own four-voice opening (six, seven, five voices respectively), supporting independence. Seed 42 is the exception: its intake window catches early worker onsets and opens anomalously high at seven voices, so its five-voice convergence is a *decrease* alongside tempo, the one run where the channels move together, not independently.

This shows distinct coordinative states produce non-arbitrary musical configurations, mapping fidelity and nothing more. It does not show a listener hears four voices versus six, can name what changed, or could do either while attending to something else. Those are questions for the listening work in Section 5.

**Key calibration discoveries.** Several decisions emerged from building and listening rather than a priori design. Voice crossing produced audible confusion, needing explicit suppression via a parallel-fifths and crossing-avoidance pass at each chord change. Bass/piano register separation was essential; overlapping registers read as conflict rather than one piece. Agent completion timing was most consequential: inferring completion from a silence timeout left voices sounding 1.8-8.2 seconds after an agent's last activity, a lagging signal on exactly the dimension the instrument exists to convey. Treating a terminal value in `gen_ai.response.finish_reasons`, the OpenTelemetry GenAI attribute for how a model call ended, as the completion signal, falling back to silence timeout only when telemetry is silent, brought that lag down to 0.8-3.2 seconds. The residual is consistent with chord-boundary quantization rather than a detectability failure. These are pipeline-specific ranges, not production latency; terminality is a heuristic on three values (`end_turn`, `max_tokens`, `stop_sequence`), not general lifecycle semantics. Inferring a transition from absence is slower and less accurate than observing it directly.

**Supplementary audio.** This submission is accompanied by rendered audio examples of the grammar in each state the prototype produces, all rendered through the batch engine described above. The clean run through intake, fan-out, and convergence is real, not hand-tuned, telemetry: the mock swarm pipeline's own derived spans (Figure 3's seed-0 run). The four implemented Tier 2 signatures, drift, collusion, a poisoned spawn, and silent failure/stall, are excerpted from the hand-authored calibration trace at their known, documented injection timestamps, not from the drift detector's own output (Section 5); we do not claim these excerpts are real telemetry. A browser demo (`demo.html`), generating a fresh, never-repeating session on each visit, is also available.

## 5. Future Directions

The grammar is implemented and the prototype is running. What follows are the directions we consider most load-bearing, not a complete research program.

**Listener validation.** The Section 3 mapping produces musically coherent, telemetry-correlated output. Whether a listener can learn to decode it, and how quickly, is untested. The corpus grounding (Sections 3-4) motivates a learnability hypothesis: a jazz-familiar listener already has the relevant statistical schema and needs only to learn what a deviation denotes. This paper does not test it. A controlled listening study, measuring detection and identification of the Tier 2 signatures against a real observability baseline, is the natural next step, the one that would turn this from a systems contribution into a validated oversight tool. We regard it as future work rather than sketch its design here, since the design depends on choices, a participant population, a training protocol, a baseline, that deserve their own treatment rather than a paragraph. A minimal informal check was out of scope here too: doing it credibly, without conflating design-time familiarity with naive listening, needs the protocol above, not a shortcut around it.

**Scaling.** A hundred agents cannot each be an audible voice; the prototype pools onto a fixed count (Figure 2). Orchestral sections, hierarchical sonification summarizing a sub-swarm as one texture, or attention-directed zoom are candidates, each trading per-agent resolution for a higher ceiling.

**A first drift detector.** The shared onset grid (Section 3) is a clock computed from tempo and form, independent of any agent's behavior, so the gap between a window's start and a voice's earliest span inside it is a real signal, not assumed. Our first-pass detector regresses each voice's deviation from the cross-voice median offset against window time, flagging one that trends upward with real correlation and lands two pooled standard deviations above the group's noise floor. Validated three ways: recovers a synthetic injected ramp once it exceeds ~4ms/window against +-30ms jitter; ~3% false-positive rate on pure-jitter controls (1/30 seeds); and on one real captured session (333 spans, 29 agents; a single coding-assistant session's own tool-call telemetry, not multi-agent swarm coordination), correctly reports no drift rather than manufacturing one. Not a validated detector; recall against genuine real-world drift is unverified, since that trace carries none by construction, and is not the target coordination pattern regardless. Extending this to collusion, poisoned spawn, and runaway loop would turn the rest into a detector runnable on live, unlabeled traffic.

**Distribution.** A hostable prototype ingesting a live OTLP stream from real deployments, not synthesized or replayed spans, is the remaining step toward a public instrument, and a more interesting evaluation environment.

## 6. Objections and Responses

**This is just sonification for monitoring.** It is the same technique (Section 2), aimed at a new target: an unbounded population's semantic and coordinative state rather than a fixed topology's physical or infrastructural one, with a grammar (Section 3) built around that target's constraints rather than borrowed from an infrastructure-metrics context. The contribution is the target and the grammar, not the invention of sonification.

**The listener will tune it out, like every other alarm.** This is the strongest objection. "We listen to music for hours" conflates aesthetic listening, active and self-paced, with vigilance monitoring, sustained detection of infrequent signals in an unchanging stream. The vigilance decrement, a systematic decline within the first half hour of watch, is one of the most robust findings in human factors (Davies & Parasuraman 1982; Warm et al. 2008). We do not claim the grammar escapes it. We claim musical structure itself, form, harmonic motion, phrase, provides intrinsic variation a flat alarm tone does not, which may partly mitigate the monotony driving the decrement. That claim is testable rather than assertable: a listening study measuring performance across session length belongs to Section 5's future work, not to a claim made here.

**Can a listener decode semantic meaning from music reliably enough to act on it?** Partially, and how far is empirical, left open to Section 5. The design bet is on discriminations a listener makes effortlessly, a wrong note, a broken rhythm, a silence; the grammar claims only what it was built to carry, not what a future study shows.

**Publishing 45ms openly lets an adversary evade it.** A real risk we do not address: a real detector (Section 5) would need to reason about staying under a known bound, not just crossing one, which this prototype's scripted injections never face. A per-deployment or randomized threshold, or a distinct signature for evasion-shaped behavior itself, are candidate mitigations; neither is implemented or evaluated here.

**It does not scale to thousands of agents.** Not naively, and the ceiling is lower: thickness saturates at the pool size (Figure 2). Above it, spawning stays audible in tempo and density but not thickness. Scaling is a named direction (Section 5), not a solved feature. The technique earns its place first in the tens-to-hundreds regime, where per-agent visual dashboards already fail.

**Who is actually listening, and what do they do when they hear a problem?** We describe a channel, not an operations procedure. The overseer we have in mind already carries responsibility for a running deployment, an operator or the on-call engineer. Detection is this paper's scope; what happens after (paging a human, halting the run, escalating) is a deployment decision this grammar does not make. It also has a hearing dependency, positioned as supplementary to the visual trace it augments (Section 2) rather than a replacement, so a d/Deaf or hard-of-hearing overseer loses nothing they had before. Saturation thrash (Figure 2, up to 415.9 forced voice-slot reassignments/minute at fanout 32) is worth naming: a voice reassigned several times a second should read as an unstable, rapidly-reassigning texture, a symptom of exceeding the pool's capacity rather than voice-leading breaking down. Whether it reads specifically as "too many agents" or merely "something is wrong" is untested, belonging with Section 5's listening work.

**Simpler alternatives would do the job.** A threshold audio alert is simpler, needs no training, and is better for any failure expressible as a threshold on a known quantity, including a thinned ensemble, thickness dropping below a count. We would not replace it there. What it cannot express is a relationship between two channels with no single crossing value on either alone. Section 4's measured convergence result, tempo returning to its opening rate while thickness does not, is relational in this sense: no threshold on either channel alone flags it, only their divergence does, which the two-channel texture makes audible without comparing two numbers. Asynchronous review, a post-run highlight reel, forfeits continuous coverage but suits long-running deployments. We claim the grammar covers one mode these alternatives do not, continuous and requiring no foveation, not that it replaces them.

**Beautiful and informative are in tension.** They are. Pleasant music may hide the signal; signal-optimized music may be unpleasant over a long listen. Navigating that tradeoff, keeping the ugliness budget (Section 3) reserved for genuine anomalies without a healthy system sounding merely correct rather than good, is part of the grammar-design problem, arguably the more interesting design question this project raises.

**Only musicians can use it.** The corpus grounding (Sections 3-4) is meant to reduce this barrier: a grammar built from real jazz statistics is closer to a schema many listeners already carry from exposure than an arbitrary mapping would be. Whether that holds, and for whom, is a question for Section 5's listening work; we do not resolve it here, only motivate the choice.

## 7. Conclusion

We have argued for a musical grammar built for a target existing musification work does not address: an unbounded, changing population of autonomous agents whose meaningful state is semantic and coordinative rather than physical. The grammar renders that population as a single evolving harmonic texture rather than one voice per agent, a design forced by the population's lack of a fixed ceiling and by the stream-segregation limit on how many independent voices a listener can track. Ensemble thickness tracks live agent count ordinally rather than as an exact readout. Divergence from mandate is dissonance; an agent slipping its leash is a voice falling out of sync with the ensemble's shared attack; coordination among agents that should be acting independently is an unexpected unison. The prototype, in two synchronized realizations, establishes that this mapping carries real structure: musical output measurably tracks the telemetry driving it, with tempo and ensemble thickness moving independently across a run's phases in three of the four checked seeds, as the form/dynamics separation predicts. It does not establish that a listener perceives or decodes that structure. We have been careful to say so rather than let the prototype's existence imply it.

Multi-agent AI coordination, illegible to a human watching a visual trace update in parallel at machine speed, is the application that motivated this grammar and makes it matter beyond a compositional exercise. Whether it can also serve that application, whether a human can learn to hear an agent system the way an experienced listener hears a choir losing its tenors, is the open question left to Section 5's future work. What this paper establishes is narrower and, we think, sufficient on its own: a jazz-grounded musical grammar for an unbounded, semantically defined source population, and a working demonstration that there is real structure in the resulting signal to eventually learn to hear.
